The Contraceptive Pill
and Your Hormones.

The contraceptive pill is the most widely prescribed medication in women's history. Most women are given a prescription and a leaflet. Very few are given a complete picture of what the pill does to their hormonal system — including testosterone, SHBG, libido, mood and the cycle that returns when they stop.

By Andreea Mighiu  ·  Zōē Women  ·  Hormonal health education

What the pill actually does to your hormonal system — the complete picture

The combined oral contraceptive pill works by delivering synthetic estrogen (ethinylestradiol) and a synthetic progestin at doses that suppress the natural HPO axis — preventing the hormonal cascade that leads to ovulation. This is pharmacologically effective as contraception. What is less consistently communicated is everything else it does to the hormonal environment.

It eliminates the natural hormonal cycle. The four phases — menstrual, follicular, ovulatory, luteal — cease to exist in their natural form. The monthly variation in estrogen, progesterone and testosterone that governs energy, libido, training response, mood and cognitive function is replaced by a stable, non-cycling synthetic hormone environment. There is no follicular phase energy rise, no ovulatory peak, no luteal phase progesterone — and no premenstrual drop, which for some women is the primary motivation for taking it.

It elevates SHBG significantly. Sex hormone binding globulin — the protein that binds and deactivates sex hormones — is elevated by the pill through liver stimulation from ethinylestradiol. Elevated SHBG reduces free testosterone and free estrogen availability at the cellular level. This is the primary mechanism behind the libido reduction, reduced genital sensitivity and motivational flatness that some women on the pill experience. Read more about how testosterone governs female libido.

It removes the ovulatory testosterone peak. The brief testosterone rise at ovulation — the monthly driver of heightened sexual desire, physical confidence and competitive drive — does not occur on the pill because ovulation is suppressed. Women who were cycling naturally before the pill lose this monthly peak without a replacement. The cumulative effect over months and years of pill use can significantly shift a woman's baseline experience of desire and physical drive.

Reference: Skovlund et al., Association of hormonal contraception with depression — JAMA Psychiatry, PubMed

Does the contraceptive pill affect hormones?
Yes — profoundly. It suppresses the natural HPO axis and replaces the cycling hormonal system with a stable synthetic one. It elevates SHBG reducing free testosterone and estrogen. It removes the ovulatory testosterone peak. It replaces natural progesterone with synthetic progestins that have a different neurological profile. The hormonal environment on the pill is fundamentally different from the natural cycle in every measurable dimension.

Coming off the pill — what to expect and how to support the transition

The post-pill period is one of the least discussed and most significant hormonal transitions a woman can experience. The body must re-establish the HPO axis from scratch — rebuilding the hormonal cascade that the pill has been suppressing. This takes time and the transition produces specific and predictable hormonal effects.

Post-pill acne. As SHBG begins to decrease after stopping, free testosterone increases. The skin — which was accustomed to suppressed free testosterone under the pill — responds to the returning androgen activity. Post-pill acne is one of the most commonly reported experiences after stopping and is hormonally explained by this mechanism. It typically resolves within two to six months as the hormonal system stabilizes.

Cycle irregularity. The HPO axis may take one to six months to restore regular ovulatory cycles. Cycle length may be variable. Ovulation may be delayed. Some women experience post-pill amenorrhea — absent periods — for up to six months. This is temporary in the vast majority of cases and does not indicate permanent fertility impact.

SHBG remaining elevated. One of the most clinically significant and least discussed post-pill effects: SHBG can remain elevated for six months or more after stopping. Free testosterone remains suppressed during this window despite no longer taking the pill. Women who stop the pill specifically to improve libido or mood may not see improvement for several months due to this persistent SHBG elevation.

Nutritional support for the transition. Zinc 25mg daily — supports testosterone synthesis and receptor function, and directly opposes SHBG elevation. Vitamin B6 50mg — supports progesterone synthesis and reduces the initial post-pill hormonal disruption. Magnesium glycinate 375mg — supports HPA axis regulation and sleep quality as natural progesterone cycles restore. Adequate dietary fat — cholesterol substrate for steroid hormone re-synthesis. Does the pill lower testosterone in women?
Yes — through SHBG elevation. The pill increases SHBG which binds and deactivates free testosterone. Total testosterone may appear normal on testing while free testosterone is significantly reduced. SHBG can remain elevated for months after stopping — meaning free testosterone suppression persists beyond discontinuation. This is the primary mechanism behind pill-related libido reduction and motivational flatness.
What happens to hormones when you stop the pill?
The HPO axis rebuilds its natural rhythm — taking weeks to months. Initial androgen surge as SHBG slowly decreases — potentially causing acne. Cycle irregularity for one to six months. Possible post-pill amenorrhea up to six months. SHBG remaining elevated for months keeping free testosterone suppressed despite stopping. Gradual return of natural cycle patterns including premenstrual symptoms the pill had masked. Nutritional support — zinc, vitamin B6, magnesium, adequate fat — supports a smoother transition.

Can the pill cause depression and mood changes?
Research suggests yes — for some women. A large Danish cohort study of over one million women found combined oral contraceptive use associated with significantly higher rates of antidepressant prescription and depression diagnosis. The mechanism likely involves the absence of naturally cycling progesterone and the different neurological profile of synthetic progestins. Individual response varies by formulation and individual neurochemistry.
Does the pill affect libido long-term?
Yes — for some women significantly. Reduced free testosterone through SHBG elevation combined with absent ovulatory testosterone peak removes two primary drivers of female sexual desire. SHBG can remain elevated for six months or more after stopping — meaning libido effects may persist beyond discontinuation. Re-establishing natural cycle function involves a period of hormonal adjustment that typically takes two to six months.
