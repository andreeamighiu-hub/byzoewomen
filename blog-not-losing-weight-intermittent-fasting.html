Why Am I Not Losing Weight
With Intermittent Fasting?

You're fasting clean, eating well, doing everything the plan said. For a while it worked. Now the scale won't move — and the obvious conclusion, that you must be doing something wrong, is the wrong one. For women, the reason fasting stalls is hormonal, and it's fixable.

By Andreea Mighiu · Zōē · byzoewomen.com

The frustrating part is that it worked at first

This is what makes a fasting plateau so confusing. For the first month or two, the scale moved, your energy was good, the protocol felt sustainable. Then it stopped — and because nothing in your effort changed, the natural conclusion is that you're failing at it. You're not. The early success and the later stall are two parts of the same hormonal story.

Fasting is a cortisol stimulus. Your body reads the absence of food as a potential energy shortage and raises cortisol to mobilize stored fuel. Early on, in a metabolically resilient phase, that works in your favor. Sustained across every phase of your cycle, the same stimulus becomes chronic — and chronic cortisol is one of the most reliable ways to stall fat loss in women.

Why fasting stalls fat loss in women specifically

Men's hormones reset on a roughly 24-hour cycle, so a fixed daily fast suits their biology. Women run a 28-day cycle, and cortisol sensitivity changes across it — lowest in the follicular phase, highest in the late luteal phase, the week before your period. Fasting the same number of hours every day means you are applying a cortisol stimulus to the most cortisol-sensitive week of your month, every single month.

Chronically elevated cortisol does three things that directly oppose fat loss. It raises blood sugar and insulin, which favors fat storage. It signals the body to store visceral fat specifically — the stubborn fat around the midsection. And at sustained high levels it suppresses the reproductive hormones that support a healthy metabolic rate. The plateau isn't a willpower problem. It's a cortisol problem.

Why am I not losing weight with intermittent fasting?
For women, the most common reason is fasting applied uniformly across the whole cycle. Fasting raises cortisol, cortisol sensitivity peaks in the late luteal phase, and fasting in that phase promotes fat storage — especially around the midsection — while suppressing the hormones fat loss depends on. The plateau signals that the schedule is fighting your cycle.

The one mistake almost every woman is making

It is fasting every day the same way, regardless of cycle phase. That single habit — the thing every generic plan tells you to do — is what turns a useful tool into a chronic stressor for the female body.

The fix is not to quit fasting. It's to time it. Restrict your longer fasts (14 to 16 hours) to the follicular phase, when cortisol sensitivity is lowest and the body handles the stimulus well. Drop to a 12 hour overnight fast through ovulation and early luteal. And stop fasting entirely in the late luteal phase and during menstruation, when your body most needs fuel and is least able to tolerate the cortisol load.

Alongside the timing, the levers that break a plateau are the unglamorous ones: enough protein, resistance training instead of more cardio, and protected sleep. More restriction and more cardio raise cortisol further — they make the exact problem worse. How do I break a fasting weight loss plateau as a woman?
Stop fasting in your late luteal phase and during menstruation, restrict longer fasts to the follicular phase, prioritize protein and resistance training over more cardio, and protect sleep. The goal is to lower the cortisol load and support reproductive hormones, not to add restriction. Matching the fasting window to cycle phase is usually what breaks the plateau.

When the plateau comes with other symptoms

If the stall arrived alongside worsening sleep, more anxiety, or changes to your period, that's confirmation the issue is hormonal rather than caloric. Those symptoms travel together because they share a cause — fasting applied without regard to cycle phase.
