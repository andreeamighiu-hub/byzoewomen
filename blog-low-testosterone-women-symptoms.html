Low Testosterone in Women:
Signs You Didn't Know Were Hormonal.

Testosterone is not a male hormone. Women produce it in the ovaries and adrenal glands and it directly governs libido, muscle mass, energy, mood and cognitive drive. When it falls — whether gradually or suddenly — the symptoms are specific, measurable and frequently misattributed to stress, aging or depression.

By Andreea Mighiu  ·  Zōē Women  ·  Hormonal health education

Why testosterone matters for women — the overlooked hormone

Testosterone is the most misunderstood hormone in women's health. It is produced in women at approximately one tenth to one twentieth of male concentrations — but its role in female physiology is not proportionally smaller. It governs libido through direct action on sexual desire circuitry. It supports muscle protein synthesis alongside estrogen. It drives the motivation, competitive drive and assertiveness that characterize the ovulatory phase. It protects bone density. It supports cognitive function and mood.

Women produce testosterone in three sites: the ovaries (approximately 25 percent), the adrenal glands (approximately 25 percent) and peripheral tissues including fat cells through aromatase conversion (approximately 50 percent). At ovulation, testosterone peaks briefly alongside estrogen — producing the characteristic mid-cycle surge in confidence, physical drive and libido that most cycling women notice without knowing its hormonal name.

When testosterone falls — whether through age, stress, contraception, overtraining or surgical intervention — the symptoms are real, specific and frequently misattributed to everything except the actual hormonal cause.

Reference: Davis et al., Testosterone for low libido in postmenopausal women — PubMed

What are the signs of low testosterone in women?
Persistent fatigue not improved by sleep. Low or absent libido — specifically the absence of spontaneous desire. Difficulty building muscle despite consistent training. Reduced strength and endurance. Brain fog and concentration difficulty. Low mood and reduced motivation. Thinning pubic and underarm hair. Reduced clitoral sensitivity. These symptoms cluster together and are frequently misattributed to stress or depression rather than their specific hormonal cause.

The most common causes — what is reducing your testosterone

The contraceptive pill. Combined hormonal contraception increases SHBG — sex hormone binding globulin — which binds free testosterone and reduces its biological availability. Some women on the pill have total testosterone levels that appear normal on testing but free testosterone that is significantly reduced. Libido reduction after starting the pill is one of the most commonly reported side effects and is pharmacologically explained by this mechanism. Some women notice the effect persists for months after stopping the pill as SHBG remains elevated.

Chronic stress and overtraining. Cortisol and testosterone compete for the same precursor molecule — pregnenolone. Under chronic stress, the body preferentially converts pregnenolone to cortisol. Testosterone production is reduced as a direct consequence. Women who are chronically overtrained — high training volume, low caloric intake, high life stress — consistently show reduced testosterone alongside other hormonal disruptions. Read more about how overtraining affects hormones in women.

Caloric restriction. Testosterone requires cholesterol for synthesis. Significant caloric restriction — particularly very low fat diets — reduces the raw material for testosterone production. The HPG axis also reads severe energy deficit as incompatible with reproduction and reduces sex hormone production including testosterone as a consequence.

Age and perimenopause. Testosterone peaks in the early 20s and declines steadily at approximately 1 to 2 percent per year. By the mid-40s most women have roughly half the testosterone they had at 20. Perimenopause accelerates this decline as ovarian function reduces. The combination of declining testosterone and declining estrogen in perimenopause produces the libido, energy and mood changes that many women experience in their 40s. Read more about how hormones change through the decades.

What causes low testosterone in women?
Natural age-related decline from the early 20s onwards. The contraceptive pill increasing SHBG and reducing free testosterone. Adrenal dysfunction from chronic stress. Overtraining combined with caloric restriction suppressing the HPG axis. Perimenopause reducing ovarian testosterone production. Surgical removal of the ovaries. Each cause requires a different primary intervention — identifying your specific cause is the first step toward addressing it effectively.
Can low testosterone cause weight gain in women?
Yes — through reduced muscle mass (lower metabolic rate) and reduced insulin sensitivity (more efficient fat storage particularly abdominally). Low testosterone makes muscle harder to build and easier to lose. It also increases insulin resistance — promoting fat storage even without increased caloric intake. The body composition consequence of chronically low testosterone is measurable and responds well to the interventions that support testosterone production.

How to support testosterone naturally — the evidence

Resistance training. The most reliably effective natural testosterone stimulus. Compound movements — squats, deadlifts, rows, presses — produce acute testosterone elevation and improve testosterone receptor sensitivity over time. Prioritizing resistance training over chronic cardio produces better testosterone outcomes and better body composition simultaneously. Read the complete guide at cycle syncing your workout plan.

Adequate dietary fat. Testosterone is synthesized from cholesterol. Low fat diets — particularly those below 20 percent of total calories from fat — measurably reduce testosterone production. Include adequate saturated fat (red meat, eggs, dairy), monounsaturated fat (olive oil, avocado) and omega-3 fatty acids (fatty fish, flaxseed) for optimal hormone synthesis.

Zinc 25mg daily. A direct cofactor in testosterone synthesis and testosterone receptor function. Active and menstruating women are at particular risk of zinc deficiency. Sources: red meat, oysters, pumpkin seeds, lentils.

Sleep protection. Testosterone is produced predominantly during deep slow-wave sleep. Even moderate sleep deprivation measurably reduces testosterone. Consistent sleep timing and duration — 7 to 9 hours — is one of the most impactful testosterone support interventions available.

Vitamin D optimization. Deficiency is associated with lower testosterone. Target 75 to 150 nmol/L. Read more about vitamin D and women's hormones.

How can women increase testosterone naturally?
Resistance training — the most effective natural stimulus. Adequate dietary fat above 25 percent of calories including saturated fat for cholesterol substrate. Zinc 25mg daily as a direct cofactor. Sleep 7 to 9 hours — testosterone is produced during deep sleep. Vitamin D optimization to 75 to 150 nmol/L. Chronic stress management to reduce cortisol competition with testosterone for pregnenolone. These address the mechanisms of testosterone production rather than symptoms alone.
How is low testosterone diagnosed in women?
Test both total testosterone and free testosterone alongside SHBG. Free testosterone — not total — reflects what is actually available at the cellular level. Test in the morning in the follicular phase of the cycle for most accurate baseline. Symptoms in context of results matter more than the absolute number — many women with symptoms fall within laboratory normal ranges that are too broad to be clinically meaningful for individual assessment.
