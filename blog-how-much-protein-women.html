How Much Protein
Should Women Eat?

The standard protein recommendation of 0.8g per kg of bodyweight was established using research conducted predominantly on men. For women who train, manage their hormonal cycle and want to build muscle or lose fat, this number is not just inadequate — it is actively counterproductive in specific phases of the cycle.

By Andreea Mighiu  ·  Zōē Women  ·  Hormonal health education

Why the standard protein recommendation fails women — the research gap

The dietary reference intake of 0.8g of protein per kg of bodyweight per day — the number that appears on nutrition labels, government guidelines and most GP advice — was not designed for women who train. It was established as the minimum required to prevent nitrogen deficiency in sedentary adults, based predominantly on research conducted on men. For active women managing a 28-day hormonal cycle, this number is insufficient in every phase and critically insufficient in the luteal phase.

The fundamental issue: protein requirements for women fluctuate across the cycle. Estrogen in the follicular phase supports muscle protein synthesis — the same protein intake produces more muscle building return in this phase than in the luteal phase. Progesterone in the luteal phase increases muscle protein catabolism — the breakdown of muscle protein for energy. A fixed daily protein target applied uniformly across all four phases either undersupports the luteal phase (causing muscle breakdown) or is unnecessary in the follicular phase.

The evidence-supported range for active women: 1.6 to 2.2g per kg of bodyweight daily, with the upper end of this range prioritized specifically in the luteal phase. This is the recommendation that reflects both the research on active women and the hormonal reality of the cycle.

Reference: Morton et al., A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength — PubMed

How much protein do women need per day?
Active women need 1.6 to 2.2g per kg of bodyweight per day — significantly more than the 0.8g standard recommendation. Women focused on muscle building or body recomposition should target the upper end. The luteal phase specifically requires higher protein as progesterone increases muscle protein catabolism. Most women eating for hormonal health are protein deficient — particularly in the phase where protein need is highest.

Protein by cycle phase — what your body needs each week

Menstrual phase (Days 1 to 5) — minimum 1.6g per kg. Iron loss through menstrual blood makes heme iron-containing proteins — red meat, eggs — particularly valuable in this phase. The body is managing inflammation and energy deficit from hormonal withdrawal. Adequate protein prevents muscle catabolism during a phase where training intensity is appropriately reduced.

Follicular phase (Days 6 to 13) — 1.8 to 2.0g per kg. Rising estrogen supports muscle protein synthesis — protein consumed in this phase produces the highest muscle building return of the cycle. Maximum training intensity in this phase combined with adequate protein produces the best body composition outcomes per unit of effort. Distribute protein across at least three meals — 30 to 40g per meal maximizes muscle protein synthesis throughout the day.

Ovulatory phase (Days 14 to 16) — 1.8 to 2.0g per kg. Peak estrogen and testosterone. Maximum anabolic environment. Adequate protein fuels the performance and muscle building capacity of the three-day ovulatory peak. Do not restrict — this is not the week to cut protein for any reason.

Luteal phase (Days 17 to 28) — 2.0 to 2.2g per kg. The highest protein requirement phase. Progesterone increases muscle protein breakdown. Metabolic rate is 200 to 300 calories higher than in the follicular phase. The combination of elevated catabolism and elevated energy expenditure requires both more total calories and more protein to maintain muscle mass. Most women who restrict calories in the luteal phase — which is common given premenstrual appetite complexity — are simultaneously at their highest protein need and their lowest protein intake. Read more about why fat loss plateaus in the luteal phase.

Should women eat more protein before their period?
Yes — the late luteal phase is the highest protein requirement phase. Progesterone increases muscle protein catabolism. Metabolic rate is elevated 200 to 300 calories above baseline. Most women simultaneously restrict calories premenstrually — creating the worst possible combination of elevated protein need and reduced protein intake. Target 2.0 to 2.2g per kg bodyweight in the late luteal phase specifically.
Does the menstrual cycle affect protein absorption?
Progesterone in the luteal phase increases protein catabolism — meaning the same protein intake produces less net muscle protein synthesis than in the follicular phase where estrogen supports synthesis. Higher protein intake in the luteal phase compensates for this reduced efficiency. The cycle does not affect protein absorption per se — it affects the efficiency with which absorbed protein is used for muscle building versus energy production.

Best protein sources for hormonal health — what to prioritize

Red meat. The most complete hormonal nutrition food. Provides complete protein, heme iron (critical for menstruating women), zinc (direct cofactor in testosterone and progesterone synthesis), B12 and creatine. Women who train and menstruate have significantly higher iron and zinc requirements than sedentary women — red meat addresses both simultaneously. Two to three servings per week is appropriate for most active women.

Eggs. Complete protein, cholesterol substrate for steroid hormone synthesis, choline for cognitive function and liver health, and zinc. One of the most nutritionally complete single foods for hormonal health in women. The cholesterol in eggs does not raise cardiovascular risk in the context of a whole food diet — and cholesterol is the direct precursor for estrogen, progesterone and testosterone synthesis.

Fatty fish. Complete protein plus omega-3 fatty acids which support prostaglandin balance — directly relevant to period pain and inflammation management. Two to three servings per week covers both protein and omega-3 requirements for most women.

Greek yogurt and cottage cheese. High protein, calcium for bone density (critical alongside the declining estrogen of perimenopause), and casein protein which provides slow-release amino acids — particularly valuable at the last meal of the day for overnight muscle protein synthesis support.

Plant sources — lentils, hemp seeds, quinoa, edamame. Adequate protein but require careful combination for complete amino acid profiles. Lower bioavailability than animal proteins — typically require 20 to 30 percent higher total intake to achieve equivalent muscle protein synthesis. Valuable as part of a varied diet but insufficient as the sole protein source for active women targeting optimal hormonal and muscle building outcomes.

Does protein affect women's hormones?
Yes — protein directly supports hormone synthesis. Peptide hormones are made from protein. Steroid hormones require amino acid cofactors. Inadequate protein reduces amino acid availability for hormone synthesis. Protein also stabilizes blood sugar — preventing insulin spikes that drive SHBG reduction and androgen elevation. Adequate protein is one of the most impactful single nutritional interventions for hormonal health in women.
What is the best protein source for hormonal health in women?
Red meat — most concentrated source of zinc and heme iron. Eggs — complete protein, cholesterol substrate, choline. Fatty fish — complete protein plus omega-3 for prostaglandin balance. Greek yogurt and cottage cheese — calcium and casein for overnight synthesis. Plant proteins require higher total intake and careful combination for equivalent muscle protein synthesis outcomes. Animal proteins provide the micronutrients most critical for female hormone synthesis alongside the protein itself.
