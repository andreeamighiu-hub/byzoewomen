Oestrogen Dominance in Women:
Symptoms, Causes and How to Fix It Naturally.

Oestrogen dominance is one of the most searched hormonal terms in women's health — and one of the most misunderstood. It is not simply having too much oestrogen. It is having too much oestrogen relative to progesterone. Here is what that means, what causes it, and what actually works.

By Andreea Mighiu · Zōē · byzoewomen.com

What oestrogen dominance actually means — the precise definition

Oestrogen does not operate in isolation. Its effects on the body depend heavily on its ratio to progesterone. When oestrogen is high and progesterone is adequate, most women feel well. When oestrogen is high and progesterone is low — or when progesterone drops faster than oestrogen at the end of the luteal phase — the relative dominance of oestrogen produces a characteristic cluster of symptoms.

This can happen in three ways. Oestrogen is genuinely elevated — from excess production, impaired clearance or external exposure. Progesterone is genuinely low — from insufficient ovulation, chronic stress or nutritional deficiency. Or both are occurring simultaneously.

What is oestrogen dominance?
Oestrogen dominance is a state in which oestrogen is elevated relative to progesterone — either because oestrogen is too high, progesterone is too low, or both. It disrupts the hormonal balance the female cycle depends on and produces a characteristic cluster of symptoms including heavy periods, PMS, mood changes, weight gain around the hips and thighs, breast tenderness and bloating.

The symptoms — and what each one means hormonally

Heavy or painful periods. Excess oestrogen stimulates the endometrium — the uterine lining — to build beyond what is needed. When progesterone cannot adequately oppose this proliferation, the lining becomes thicker and the period heavier. The same imbalance increases prostaglandin production, which drives period pain.

Premenstrual mood symptoms. Excess oestrogen relative to progesterone in the luteal phase disrupts the GABA and serotonin systems that progesterone normally supports. The emotional reactivity, anxiety and irritability of PMS are amplified significantly when oestrogen dominance is present.

Weight gain around the hips, thighs and abdomen. Oestrogen stimulates fat storage in specific depots — the hips, thighs and lower abdomen. Elevated oestrogen also promotes water retention and can contribute to insulin resistance. This fat pattern is often described as feeling impossible to shift — and in an oestrogen-dominant state it largely is, because the hormonal environment is actively promoting its storage.

Breast tenderness. One of the most consistent symptoms of elevated oestrogen. Progesterone has a direct anti-proliferative effect on breast tissue — when it cannot adequately oppose oestrogen, breast tissue becomes more sensitive and tender, particularly in the premenstrual week.

What are the symptoms of oestrogen dominance?
Heavy or painful periods, premenstrual mood changes, weight gain around hips and thighs, breast tenderness, bloating in the second half of the cycle, low libido, fatigue, brain fog and irregular cycles. These symptoms cluster in the luteal phase when progesterone should be balancing oestrogen but cannot do so adequately.

What causes it — the five main mechanisms

Impaired liver oestrogen clearance. The liver is the primary route by which oestrogen is metabolised and prepared for excretion. When liver function is compromised — by alcohol, poor nutrition, excess saturated fat or toxic load — oestrogen is not cleared efficiently and recirculates. Alcohol is the most significant dietary contributor: even moderate drinking (1 to 2 units daily) measurably elevates circulating oestrogen by reducing liver clearance capacity.

Source: Gavaler & Van Thiel, The association between moderate alcoholic beverage consumption and serum estradiol and testosterone levels in normal postmenopausal women — PubMed.

Poor gut microbiome health. The gut microbiome plays a direct role in oestrogen metabolism through a collection of bacteria called the estrobolome. These bacteria produce beta-glucuronidase — an enzyme that can reactivate conjugated oestrogen and return it to circulation rather than allowing it to be excreted. Poor gut health dysregulates this process, effectively recycling oestrogen rather than eliminating it. This is why gut health is directly relevant to hormonal balance — not through a vague wellness connection but through a specific metabolic mechanism.

Chronic cortisol elevation. Cortisol and progesterone share the same receptor sites. Chronically elevated cortisol competes with progesterone for those receptors, effectively reducing progesterone's activity without changing its blood level. This is functional progesterone deficiency — a state in which progesterone appears normal on a blood test but cannot adequately oppose oestrogen because cortisol is occupying its receptors. Read more about how cortisol disrupts the hormonal cycle.

Xenoestrogen exposure. Xenoestrogens are environmental compounds that mimic oestrogen in the body — including BPA from plastics, phthalates from personal care products, parabens from cosmetics and certain pesticide residues. They bind to oestrogen receptors and activate them without being actual oestrogen, effectively adding to total oestrogenic load.

Excess body fat. Adipose tissue (body fat) converts androgens to oestrogen through an enzyme called aromatase. Higher body fat increases aromatase activity and therefore oestrogen production — which is why weight loss is both a consequence and a cause of oestrogen dominance.

What causes oestrogen dominance in women?
The five main mechanisms: impaired liver oestrogen clearance (alcohol is the single biggest dietary contributor), poor gut microbiome health disrupting oestrogen excretion, chronic cortisol competing with progesterone for receptor sites, xenoestrogen exposure from plastics and personal care products, and excess body fat increasing aromatase-driven oestrogen production.

How to reduce oestrogen dominance naturally — the evidence

Cruciferous vegetables and DIM. Broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts and kale contain indole-3-carbinol, which the liver converts to DIM (diindolylmethane). DIM supports the liver's oestrogen metabolism pathways — specifically promoting conversion to less potent oestrogen metabolites rather than more potent ones. Three to five servings per week of cruciferous vegetables, particularly in the follicular phase, directly supports healthy oestrogen clearance. Read more in the cycle syncing diet guide.

Magnesium glycinate. Magnesium is a cofactor in phase two liver detoxification — the pathway responsible for conjugating oestrogen before excretion. Magnesium deficiency (extremely common in women) directly impairs this process. At 375mg daily, magnesium glycinate supports oestrogen clearance alongside its well-documented benefits for mood, sleep and PMS symptoms.

Adequate dietary fibre. Oestrogen that has been conjugated by the liver is excreted via bile into the gut. Here, it should bind to fibre and be eliminated in stool. Without adequate fibre, beta-glucuronidase activity deconjugates the oestrogen, allowing it to be reabsorbed rather than excreted. 25 to 35g of dietary fibre daily — from vegetables, legumes, oats and seeds — supports oestrogen excretion directly.

Reducing alcohol. Even moderate alcohol consumption measurably elevates circulating oestrogen. In an oestrogen-dominant state, alcohol is the single most impactful dietary change. Reducing or eliminating alcohol — particularly in the luteal phase when the oestrogen-progesterone ratio is already under pressure — produces measurable improvements within one to two cycles.

Managing cortisol. Reducing the cortisol load that competes with progesterone — through cycle-aware training, consistent sleep, stress reduction and magnesium — functionally improves progesterone's ability to balance oestrogen without changing oestrogen levels at all.

How do you reduce oestrogen dominance naturally?
Cruciferous vegetables 3 to 5 times per week for DIM-supported liver oestrogen metabolism. Magnesium glycinate 375mg daily for phase two liver detoxification. 25 to 35g dietary fibre daily for oestrogen excretion. Reducing or eliminating alcohol — the single most impactful dietary change. Managing cortisol load through cycle-aware training and sleep protection. These address the specific mechanisms of oestrogen dominance rather than its symptoms.
