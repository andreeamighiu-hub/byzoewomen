HRT and Perimenopause:
Is It Safe and What Does the Evidence Say?

Hormone replacement therapy is simultaneously one of the most searched, most feared and most misunderstood medical interventions in women's health. The fear comes from a 2002 study that has since been largely reinterpreted. The evidence in 2026 paints a significantly more nuanced picture — and for many women in perimenopause, HRT is the most effective treatment available.

By Andreea Mighiu  ·  Zōē Women  ·  Hormonal health education

What the 2002 WHI study actually showed — and why it was misinterpreted

The widespread fear of HRT that persists among women and many clinicians traces back to a single study: the Women's Health Initiative, published in 2002. The WHI was halted early after appearing to show increased breast cancer, cardiovascular disease and stroke risk in women taking combined HRT. Prescriptions dropped by more than 50 percent in the years that followed. Women who needed treatment stopped taking it. Clinicians became reluctant to prescribe it.

What has since become clear in the research is that the WHI studied a population that was not representative of women typically prescribed HRT. The average age of participants was 63 — most were significantly past the menopause transition. Many already had subclinical cardiovascular disease at the time of enrollment. The HRT used was oral conjugated equine estrogen combined with medroxyprogesterone acetate — synthetic hormones with different pharmacological properties than the transdermal bioidentical forms now most commonly used. Subsequent re-analysis of the WHI data for younger women — under 60 and within ten years of menopause — showed no increased cardiovascular risk and a possible cardiovascular protective effect (PubMed).

The 'timing hypothesis' that emerged from this re-analysis: HRT started close to menopause onset in healthy women appears to be protective for the cardiovascular system. HRT started years after menopause, in women already past the window of estrogen's protective effects on arterial health, shows a different and more complex risk picture. The 2002 WHI studied the latter and the results were applied to the former — producing a population-wide withdrawal of effective treatment from the women who would have benefited most from it.

Is HRT safe for perimenopause?
For most healthy women under 60 within ten years of menopause onset, current evidence supports HRT benefits outweighing risks. The 2002 WHI study has been substantially reinterpreted — it studied older women with pre-existing cardiovascular risk using oral synthetic hormones rather than transdermal bioidentical forms. Current guidance from the British Menopause Society and North American Menopause Society supports HRT as the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms with an acceptable risk profile in appropriate candidates.
What does HRT do for perimenopause?
Replaces declining estrogen and progesterone — reducing or eliminating hot flushes, night sweats, sleep disruption, vaginal symptoms, mood changes, cognitive symptoms and providing bone density and cardiovascular protection when started close to menopause onset. The most consistently effective treatment available for moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms and perimenopause-related quality of life reduction.

Forms of HRT — what the evidence supports in 2026

Transdermal estrogen — the evidence-based first choice. Patches, gels and creams deliver estrogen directly through the skin into the bloodstream — bypassing first-pass liver metabolism. This is the key pharmacological difference from oral estrogen. Oral estrogen stimulates liver production of clotting factors — producing a small but measurable increased blood clot risk. Transdermal estrogen does not stimulate the liver in this way and does not carry this blood clot risk increase. For women with any cardiovascular concern, transdermal is the consistently preferred route. In February 2026, the FDA updated HRT labeling language to reflect evolving evidence on hormone therapy risks and benefits.

Micronized progesterone — the preferred progestogen. Women with a uterus require progesterone alongside estrogen to protect the endometrial lining. The evidence increasingly supports micronized progesterone (identical to the body's own progesterone, sold as Utrogestan) over synthetic progestins. Synthetic progestins — medroxyprogesterone acetate, norethisterone — have different receptor binding profiles from natural progesterone and appear to carry higher breast cancer risk. Micronized progesterone has the most favorable risk profile, and its neurological profile more closely matches natural progesterone — supporting sleep and mood rather than having the disrupting effects of some synthetic progestins.

Testosterone for women. Testosterone is increasingly prescribed alongside estrogen-progesterone HRT for women with persisting low libido, fatigue or cognitive symptoms after estrogen therapy is optimized. The evidence base for testosterone in women has strengthened significantly — the British Menopause Society explicitly supports testosterone as an evidence-based option for hypoactive sexual desire disorder in perimenopausal and menopausal women.

Important: This article is educational and does not constitute medical advice. Decisions about hormone replacement therapy should be made with a qualified healthcare provider who can assess your individual history, symptoms and circumstances. HRT is not appropriate for everyone and requires clinical evaluation.

What is the safest form of HRT?
Transdermal estrogen (patch, gel, cream) combined with micronized progesterone (for women with a uterus). Transdermal estrogen bypasses liver first-pass metabolism — avoiding the blood clot risk associated with oral estrogen. Micronized progesterone has a more favorable risk profile than synthetic progestins for breast cancer risk and a neurological profile that more closely matches natural progesterone.
When should I start HRT for perimenopause?
The evidence most strongly supports starting within ten years of menopause onset and before age 60 — the 'timing hypothesis' window where cardiovascular protection rather than risk is the more likely outcome. Starting in perimenopause when symptoms first appear and before significant cardiovascular or bone density change has occurred produces the most favorable risk-benefit ratio.
What are the risks of HRT?
Type and route dependent: oral estrogen carries small blood clot risk — transdermal does not. Combined HRT with synthetic progestins carries small breast cancer risk — micronized progesterone appears lower risk. Women with history of breast cancer, blood clots, stroke, liver disease require individual clinical assessment. Discussing personal risk factors with a menopause specialist is the appropriate starting point for any HRT decision.
