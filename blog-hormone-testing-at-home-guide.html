How to Test Your Hormones at Home:
The Complete Women's Guide.

Women's hormonal health has long been the territory of referrals, waiting lists and single snapshot blood tests that miss far more than they capture. That is changing. At-home hormone testing is now more accurate, more comprehensive and more actionable than it has ever been. Here is everything you need to know to use it well.

By Andreea Mighiu · Zōē · byzoewomen.com

Why home hormone testing matters — and what the clinic misses

A single blood test at the GP, taken on a random day of the cycle, interpreted against a broad population reference range — this is the standard of hormonal investigation that most women receive. The problems with this approach are significant: hormones fluctuate dramatically across the cycle, a single measurement captures one moment in an inherently dynamic system, and population reference ranges are wide enough to miss clinically meaningful imbalances in the low-normal or high-normal range.

At-home testing addresses several of these limitations. It allows women to test at the right point in their cycle, to test multiple times across the cycle to capture the hormonal arc rather than a single point, and to access advanced formats — particularly the DUTCH dried urine test — that provide hormone metabolite data unavailable from standard blood panels. The result is a more complete picture, in the hands of the person it matters most to.

Can you test your hormones at home?
Yes. At-home hormone testing has become significantly more accessible and clinically meaningful over the past five years. The two most useful modalities are dried blood spot (finger prick onto a collection card) and dried urine testing. These formats provide accurate readings for estrogen, progesterone, testosterone, DHEA, cortisol, FSH, LH, and thyroid markers — and the DUTCH test additionally provides hormone metabolites, which tell you not just how much of a hormone you have but how your body is processing and clearing it.

When to test — timing is everything

The most common mistake in hormone testing is testing at the wrong point in the cycle. Hormones that are perfectly normal on day 10 may be problematic on day 21 — and vice versa. The cycle day on which you test determines what each result means.

When should women test their hormones?
Timing within the cycle matters significantly for sex hormone testing. Estrogen and LH are best tested on day 3 of the cycle. Progesterone is best tested on day 21 in a 28-day cycle — approximately 7 days after ovulation — when it should be at its peak. FSH is tested on day 3 alongside estrogen. Testosterone can be tested on day 3 alongside the other baseline markers. Cortisol via urine or saliva is tested across the full day with 4 samples regardless of cycle day.

The DUTCH test — why it is the most comprehensive option

The DUTCH test (Dried Urine Test for Comprehensive Hormones) is the most clinically detailed at-home hormone testing format currently available. What distinguishes it from blood testing is its metabolite data — not just the level of each hormone, but the pathway by which it is being broken down and cleared from the body.

Estrogen metabolites. Estrogen is metabolised through several pathways in the liver. The DUTCH test shows which pathway dominates. The 2-hydroxyestrone pathway is considered protective — it produces weaker estrogen metabolites. The 16-hydroxyestrone and 4-hydroxyestrone pathways produce more estrogenically active metabolites associated with higher inflammatory potential. For women with suspected estrogen dominance, fibroids, endometriosis, or a family history of hormone-sensitive conditions, this metabolite data is clinically significant in a way that a serum estradiol level simply is not. Read more about estrogen dominance and what drives it.

Cortisol rhythm. The DUTCH test captures free cortisol across the full day through multiple urine collections, providing a cortisol curve that reveals adrenal function patterns unavailable from a single morning blood cortisol. It also measures cortisol metabolites, which can indicate whether cortisol production is normal but clearance is abnormal — a clinically distinct pattern from true adrenal insufficiency. Read more about cortisol patterns and their hormonal downstream effects.

Methylation markers. The DUTCH test includes markers of B vitamin status and methylation function, which affect estrogen clearance. This is particularly relevant for women on hormonal contraceptives, which deplete B vitamins, or women with MTHFR variants affecting methylation.

What does the DUTCH test show?
The DUTCH test measures estrogen, progesterone, testosterone and cortisol — and critically, their metabolites. For estrogen, it shows which metabolic pathway it is taking during clearance. Some estrogen metabolites are protective; others are associated with higher estrogenic activity and inflammatory potential. This metabolite data is not available from blood testing alone, which is what makes the DUTCH test the most comprehensive at-home option for women with suspected estrogen dominance, hormone metabolism issues, or unexplained symptoms.
A blood test tells you how much of a hormone is present at one moment. The DUTCH test tells you what your body is doing with it — and that distinction changes everything about how you respond.

Blood vs saliva vs urine — which format for what

Blood (serum or dried blood spot): The gold standard for FSH, LH, and thyroid markers. Required for accurate ovarian reserve assessment. Provides total hormone levels — bound and unbound. Best for a comprehensive baseline panel run at the right cycle days.

Saliva: Measures free (unbound) hormones. Most clinically useful for cortisol, where the free fraction is the biologically active one and the stress of a blood draw can artificially spike results. Saliva cortisol across 4 time points is a practical and reliable assessment of the daily cortisol rhythm.

Dried urine (DUTCH): The most comprehensive option for sex hormones and cortisol combined. Adds metabolite data. Best for women with unexplained symptoms, suspected estrogen dominance, history of hormone-sensitive conditions, or anyone who has already run basic blood panels and wants deeper insight.

What is the difference between blood and saliva hormone testing?
Blood testing measures total hormone levels and is required for accurate FSH, LH and thyroid assessment. It gives a snapshot at the moment of collection. Saliva testing measures free (unbound) hormones — the biologically active fraction — which is more relevant for cortisol. Dried urine testing (DUTCH) captures hormone output over a longer collection window and adds metabolite data unavailable from blood or saliva. The right choice depends on what you are trying to understand.

How to interpret results — what the numbers are and are not telling you

The most important principle in hormone test interpretation is that reference ranges are population-based, not optimal-based. A result in the "normal" range means you fall within the middle 95% of a population that includes people with significant hormonal symptoms. "Normal" does not mean "well." Many women with clear clinical symptoms have results that fall just within the normal range — dismissed as fine on the basis of a number that tells them nothing about how that number is serving them.

The most useful approach to interpretation is pattern-based rather than single-value-based: how does your estrogen on day 3 compare to your progesterone on day 21? Is the estrogen-to-progesterone ratio consistent with adequate luteal function? Is your cortisol rhythm coherent — high in the morning, declining through the day — or is it flat, inverted, or spiking at night? These patterns tell a richer story than any single data point.
