Gut Health and Hormones:
The Estrobolome Explained.

The connection between gut health and women's hormones is one of the most significant developments in reproductive endocrinology research in the last decade. A specific subset of gut bacteria — the estrobolome — directly regulates how estrogen is metabolized, cleared and recirculated in the body. What is in your gut is directly affecting your hormone levels.

By Andreea Mighiu  ·  Zōē Women  ·  Hormonal health education

The estrobolome — how gut bacteria regulate your estrogen

Estrogen is not simply produced by the ovaries and used by target tissues in a linear pathway. It cycles through the liver, the intestine and back into circulation — and at each step, gut bacteria are influencing how much estrogen ultimately reaches its receptors in the brain, breast tissue, uterus and bone.

The process: the liver processes used estrogen by attaching glucuronic acid — a process called conjugation — which deactivates it and prepares it for excretion through bile into the intestine. In a healthy gut with a balanced estrobolome, most of this conjugated estrogen passes through the intestine and is excreted in stool. Problem solved.

In a disrupted gut with overgrowth of beta-glucuronidase-producing bacteria, the enzyme cleaves the glucuronic acid bond — deconjugating the estrogen and reactivating it. This reactivated estrogen is then reabsorbed from the intestine back into circulation. The liver must process it again. The cycle repeats. The net effect is consistently elevated circulating estrogen — not because the ovaries are producing too much, but because the gut is returning processed estrogen to circulation rather than excreting it.

Reference: Baker et al., Estrogen-gut microbiome axis — estrobolome and influence on hormonal cancers — PubMed

What is the estrobolome?
The subset of gut bacteria capable of metabolizing estrogen through beta-glucuronidase production. A balanced estrobolome supports efficient estrogen clearance. A disrupted estrobolome increases beta-glucuronidase activity — deconjugating processed estrogen and returning it to circulation rather than allowing excretion. The result is elevated circulating estrogen contributing to estrogen dominance, worsened PMS and heavier periods without any change in ovarian estrogen production.
How does gut health affect women's hormones?
Primarily through the estrobolome — gut bacteria regulate circulating estrogen levels by controlling whether processed estrogen is excreted or returned to circulation. A disrupted estrobolome elevates circulating estrogen. Additionally, gut health affects cortisol through the gut-brain axis, serotonin production (90 percent of serotonin is produced in the gut), and inflammation which directly disrupts ovarian hormone production. Gut health is a foundational component of female hormonal health.

The gut-cycle connection — how your cycle affects your gut

The hormonal cycle affects gut function — and gut function affects the hormonal cycle. The relationship is bidirectional and explains several common experiences that women have with digestive symptoms across the month.

Progesterone and digestion. Progesterone has a smooth muscle relaxant effect — including on the muscles of the digestive tract. In the luteal phase as progesterone rises, intestinal transit time slows. Food moves more slowly through the gut. This produces the bloating, constipation and digestive sluggishness that many women notice in the second half of their cycle. As progesterone falls just before menstruation, transit time accelerates — producing the loose stools and digestive urgency that many women experience in the first days of their period (alongside prostaglandin effects on the intestinal smooth muscle).

Estrogen and gut permeability. Estrogen supports tight junction integrity in the gut epithelium — reducing gut permeability. In the late luteal phase as estrogen falls, gut permeability can increase slightly — potentially contributing to increased food sensitivity and bloating in this phase. Women with existing gut issues often notice their symptoms worsen premenstrually through this mechanism.

Serotonin and the gut-cycle axis. Approximately 90 percent of the body's serotonin is produced in the gut. The gut microbiome supports serotonin synthesis through tryptophan metabolism. When estrogen falls in the late luteal phase and serotonin support decreases centrally, gut serotonin production is also affected — compounding the neurochemical mood and appetite changes of the premenstrual week. Supporting gut health directly supports the serotonin availability that estrogen withdrawal reduces. Read more about PMS and the neurochemical mechanisms.

Can gut health affect PMS?
Yes — through the estrobolome increasing circulating estrogen (worsening estrogen dominance symptoms), through gut serotonin production (90 percent of serotonin produced in the gut — gut health directly affects the serotonin availability that falls with estrogen premenstrually), and through gut permeability increasing in the late luteal phase worsening food sensitivities and bloating. Supporting gut health consistently improves PMS within two to three cycles in many women.
Does diet affect women's hormones through the gut?
Yes — high fiber binds intestinal estrogen preventing reabsorption, directly opposing the estrobolome reabsorption mechanism. Cruciferous vegetables provide DIM supporting liver estrogen metabolism. Fermented foods introduce bacteria supporting a healthy estrobolome. Processed food, excess sugar and alcohol disrupt microbiome diversity and increase gut permeability — worsening estrobolome function and estrogen metabolism simultaneously. Dietary fiber is the most evidence-backed single intervention for estrogen metabolism through the gut.

Supporting gut health for hormonal balance — what actually works

Dietary fiber — the primary intervention. Soluble fiber — oats, legumes, flaxseed, apples — feeds beneficial bacteria and supports microbiome diversity. Insoluble fiber — particularly from cruciferous vegetables — binds estrogen in the intestine and promotes its excretion rather than reabsorption. Minimum 25 to 35g of total dietary fiber daily from varied food sources. This is the most consistently evidence-backed single dietary intervention for estrogen metabolism through the gut.

Cruciferous vegetables daily. Broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, kale and cabbage provide DIM — diindolylmethane — which supports the liver's phase two estrogen metabolism, producing less stimulatory estrogen metabolites. They also provide the insoluble fiber that binds intestinal estrogen for excretion. Including at least one serving of cruciferous vegetables daily consistently supports both liver and gut estrogen clearance pathways.

Fermented foods. Yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi and kombucha introduce live bacteria that support microbiome diversity and estrobolome function. Two to three servings daily of fermented foods — alongside adequate prebiotic fiber — produces measurable improvements in microbiome diversity within two to four weeks. The fiber provides the substrate the bacteria need to thrive.

Reduce the estrobolome disruptors. Antibiotics — which broadly disrupt the microbiome — have a predictable effect on estrogen metabolism in the weeks following a course. Alcohol disrupts gut microbiome composition and increases gut permeability. Processed food and high sugar diets shift the microbiome toward less diverse, more pathogenic compositions that increase beta-glucuronidase activity. None of these need to be eliminated entirely — but their impact on estrogen metabolism is real and worth understanding.

What probiotics are good for women's hormones?
Lactobacillus acidophilus and Lactobacillus reuteri have the most consistent evidence for estrogen metabolism support. Bifidobacterium longum supports liver estrogen clearance. Probiotic supplements work best alongside adequate prebiotic fiber — without fiber the introduced bacteria cannot sustain themselves. Dietary fiber is the most evidence-backed single intervention for estrobolome function — probiotics are a complement, not a replacement, for fiber adequacy.
