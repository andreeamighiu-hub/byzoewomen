Training & Hormones

Why am I not seeing
results from training hard?

You are showing up, training hard, eating well, doing everything the plan says — and the results either are not coming, or they show up and then quietly disappear. The fast answer: your plan is very likely built for a body that does not have a monthly hormonal cycle, and yours does. That is exactly why Zōē exists. Most training and nutrition advice is calibrated to male physiology, which runs on a steady 24-hour rhythm. Female physiology runs on a second, longer rhythm on top of that — and it changes how your body actually absorbs the same training, week to week.

By Andreea Mighiu  ·  Training & Hormones  ·  byzoewomen.com

The real reason effort doesn't always equal results

Most training advice assumes a flat, unchanging hormonal baseline — the same intensity should produce the same adaptation every single week. For women, that assumption is wrong. Estrogen and progesterone rise and fall across a roughly month-long cycle, and both hormones directly influence how your body handles training stress: how much cortisol a session produces, how efficiently your muscles repair, even how much fuel is available for hard efforts on a given day.

This means the exact same workout, at the exact same intensity, genuinely does not produce the same result every week of your cycle. Train against that reality without knowing it exists, and you can end up doing everything "right" while your body absorbs it inconsistently — sometimes adapting well, sometimes barely recovering at all.

Why am I not seeing results even though I train hard and eat well?
The most common overlooked reason is that your training plan doesn't account for your hormonal cycle. Estrogen and progesterone shift weekly, changing how well your body recovers from and adapts to the same training load — so a plan that treats every week identically can produce inconsistent results despite consistent effort.

How your cycle changes what your body can absorb from training

In the follicular phase — roughly the two weeks after your period — rising estrogen improves insulin sensitivity, supports faster muscle repair, and generally makes your body more receptive to harder training. This is when progressive overload tends to actually stick.

In the luteal phase, progesterone rises and has a mildly catabolic, higher-cortisol effect. Recovery slows, water retention increases, and the same session that felt manageable two weeks earlier can leave you far more depleted. Training exactly as hard here, without adjusting volume or intensity, is often where "I did everything right and still didn't progress" actually comes from.

Your effort was never the problem. The plan not accounting for your biology was.

What changes when your plan is built around your hormones

Phase-aware training does not mean training less. It means shifting when you push for progressive overload and when you protect recovery, so the hard weeks actually convert into progress instead of quietly compounding fatigue. At Zōē, this is the starting point of every plan — not a generic split repeated indefinitely, but a structure that moves with your actual cycle.

Can hormones really affect my training results that much?
Yes. Estrogen and progesterone influence insulin sensitivity, cortisol response, fluid retention and muscle repair speed — all of which directly affect how your body responds to training. The effect is real, though it varies between individuals, which is why personalized tracking matters more than applying a rigid rule to every woman.
