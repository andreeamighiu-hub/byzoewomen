How to Increase Testosterone
Naturally as a Woman.

Testosterone is the hormone most directly responsible for libido, muscle building capacity, physical drive and the confidence peak of mid-cycle. Understanding how to support it naturally — through training, nutrition and lifestyle — is one of the most impactful things an active woman can do for her performance, body composition and wellbeing.

By Andreea Mighiu  ·  Zōē Women  ·  Hormonal health education

Why testosterone support matters for active women — the performance case

Every woman has a monthly testosterone peak — at ovulation, when testosterone rises briefly alongside estrogen. This peak produces the increased physical strength, higher pain tolerance, greater confidence and heightened sexual drive that characterize the three days around ovulation. It is not coincidence. It is testosterone doing exactly what it is designed to do.

Understanding how to support testosterone production means understanding how to make the most of this monthly peak — and how to maintain adequate baseline testosterone throughout the cycle for the muscle building, metabolic rate protection and motivation that testosterone provides continuously.

The good news for women who train: resistance training is simultaneously the most effective natural testosterone stimulus and the training approach that produces the best hormonal outcomes across every other aspect of the female hormonal system. Supporting testosterone and optimizing body composition are the same programme.

Does exercise increase testosterone in women?
Yes — specifically resistance training. Compound movements involving large muscle groups produce the largest acute testosterone response. High-intensity cardio also elevates testosterone briefly but chronic steady-state cardio can suppress it through cortisol. The follicular and ovulatory phases are most productive for high-intensity resistance training — testosterone is naturally elevated and the hormonal environment most supports the anabolic response.

The nutrition approach — what to eat to support testosterone

Dietary fat is non-negotiable. Testosterone is a steroid hormone synthesized from cholesterol. Without adequate dietary fat — particularly saturated and monounsaturated fat — the raw material for testosterone production is limited. Women on aggressive low-fat diets consistently show lower testosterone than those eating adequate fat. Minimum 25 to 30 percent of total calories from fat. Include red meat, eggs, dairy, olive oil and avocado regularly.

Zinc at every meal. Red meat, shellfish (oysters have the highest zinc content of any food), pumpkin seeds, lentils and hemp seeds. If dietary zinc is consistently inadequate, supplement at 25mg daily. Zinc deficiency is more common in women who train and menstruate than most standard nutritional advice acknowledges.

Pomegranate. One of the few foods with direct research showing measurable testosterone increases after consumption — a study at Queen Margaret University found a 24 percent increase in salivary testosterone after two weeks of pomegranate juice consumption. The mechanism is likely via its effects on aromatase enzyme activity.

Cruciferous vegetables. Broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts contain DIM — diindolylmethane — which supports healthy estrogen metabolism. Proper estrogen clearance indirectly supports testosterone availability by preventing excessive aromatization of testosterone to estrogen in peripheral tissues. Read more about estrogen dominance and how to address it.

What foods increase testosterone in women?
Red meat and shellfish — highest dietary zinc sources, direct cofactor in testosterone synthesis. Eggs — cholesterol substrate and zinc. Cruciferous vegetables — support estrogen metabolism, indirectly supporting testosterone availability. Pomegranate — measurable testosterone increases in research. Olive oil and avocado — monounsaturated fats for hormone synthesis. Fatty fish — omega-3 supports free testosterone availability by reducing SHBG.
Does zinc increase testosterone in women?
Yes — zinc is a direct enzyme cofactor in the testosterone biosynthesis pathway. Deficiency is associated with reduced testosterone. Supplementation at 25mg daily produces measurable improvements in women who are deficient. Active, menstruating women are at particular risk of deficiency through sweat loss and menstrual depletion. Zinc is one of the most impactful single nutritional interventions for testosterone support with the strongest evidence base.

Training, sleep and stress — the lifestyle triad

Resistance training — the most powerful lever. Three compound resistance sessions per week — squats, deadlifts, rows, overhead press, hip hinges — produce the most significant natural testosterone stimulus available. Program progressive overload in the follicular and ovulatory phases when testosterone is naturally elevated and the body is most responsive. Reduce intensity in the late luteal phase when cortisol sensitivity peaks and recovery capacity is reduced. Read the complete training guide at cycle syncing your workout plan.

Sleep — testosterone's primary production window. The majority of daily testosterone production occurs during slow-wave (deep) sleep. A single night of poor sleep measurably reduces testosterone the following day. Chronic sleep deprivation produces sustained testosterone reduction that no supplement can compensate for. Consistent sleep timing — the same wake time every day — regulates the cortisol awakening response and supports the sleep architecture that testosterone production depends on.

Cortisol management. Cortisol and testosterone compete for pregnenolone — the shared precursor molecule. Under chronic stress, pregnenolone is preferentially directed toward cortisol production at the expense of testosterone synthesis. Every cortisol management intervention — sleep, cycle-aware training intensity, adequate calories, magnesium — is simultaneously a testosterone support intervention. Read more about how cortisol disrupts hormonal health.

How can women boost testosterone naturally?
Compound resistance training three times weekly. Adequate dietary fat above 25 percent of calories. Zinc 25mg daily. Sleep 7 to 9 hours with consistent timing. Vitamin D optimization to 75 to 150 nmol/L. Cortisol management through cycle-aware training and stress reduction. These address the mechanisms of testosterone production rather than symptoms alone. Most interventions produce measurable changes within 8 to 12 weeks of consistent application.
How long does it take to increase testosterone naturally?
Zinc supplementation shows measurable changes within 4 to 6 weeks in deficient women. Resistance training produces cumulative improvements in testosterone sensitivity over 8 to 12 weeks. Sleep improvement produces measurable changes within weeks. The timeline depends on the specific cause of low testosterone and whether the intervention targets that cause directly.
