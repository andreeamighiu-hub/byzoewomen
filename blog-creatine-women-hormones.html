Creatine for Women:
Hormonal Benefits, Cycle Timing and What the Research Actually Shows.

Creatine is one of the most researched supplements in existence and, until recently, almost entirely studied in men. That is changing. Here is what the emerging research on creatine for women actually says — and why it matters far beyond the gym.

By Andreea Mighiu · Zōē · byzoewomen.com

Why creatine became a women's health conversation — and why now

For most of its research history, creatine was studied almost exclusively in male athletes. The assumption was that creatine's primary benefit — supporting high-intensity muscle performance — was less relevant to women. That assumption has not held up. As the science has broadened, three things have become clear: women have significantly lower baseline creatine stores than men, women respond to creatine supplementation differently across the cycle, and the benefits of creatine for women extend well beyond muscle performance into brain function, mood and hormonal health across the lifespan.

What creatine actually is — the mechanism

Creatine is a naturally occurring compound produced in the liver, kidneys and pancreas from the amino acids arginine, glycine and methionine. It is stored primarily in skeletal muscle as phosphocreatine, where it functions as a rapid energy buffer — regenerating ATP (the cell's primary energy currency) during short bursts of high-intensity activity.

Women produce and store less creatine than men. Baseline muscle creatine concentrations in women are approximately 70 to 80% of male levels, which means the relative gap between natural stores and optimal levels is larger — and the potential benefit from supplementation proportionally greater.

Creatine is also found in food — primarily red meat and fish. A 200g serving of salmon provides roughly 0.9g of creatine. To reach the 3 to 5g per day that research associates with meaningful benefit, supplementation is the only practical route for most people, particularly those who eat little or no meat.

Should women take creatine?
Yes. The research on creatine for women is increasingly strong — particularly for muscle retention, cognitive function, and managing hormonal transitions like perimenopause. Women have lower baseline creatine stores than men (approximately 70 to 80% of male levels), which means the relative benefit from supplementation is often greater. Creatine monohydrate at 3 to 5g daily is the most evidence-backed form and is considered safe for long-term use.

Creatine and female hormones — what the relationship actually looks like

Creatine does not directly alter estrogen or progesterone levels. What it does is support the cellular energy systems that influence how hormones are produced, transported and used — which matters more than the distinction sounds.

Estrogen and creatine share metabolic overlaps. Estrogen upregulates key enzymes in creatine synthesis and transport. In the follicular phase, when estrogen is rising, women's creatine utilisation and phosphocreatine resynthesis rates are higher. In the luteal phase, progesterone's effects on metabolism slow phosphocreatine resynthesis — meaning recovery from high-intensity exercise is measurably slower in the second half of the cycle. Consistent creatine supplementation partially compensates for this luteal-phase reduction.

Creatine and cortisol. Creatine supplementation has been shown to reduce cortisol elevation in response to high-intensity training. Since chronically elevated cortisol directly disrupts hormonal balance — competing with progesterone for receptor sites and suppressing ovulation — anything that blunts the cortisol response to training is indirectly protective of the hormonal environment. Perimenopause and creatine. As estrogen declines in perimenopause, its protective role in muscle energy metabolism is reduced. Creatine supplementation in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women has shown meaningful improvements in muscle mass, strength and cognitive function — filling part of the gap that declining estrogen leaves. This is one of the most clinically significant emerging applications of creatine for women.

Source: Smith-Ryan et al., Creatine Supplementation in Women's Health: A Lifespan Perspective — Nutrients, 2021.

Does creatine affect female hormones?
Creatine does not directly alter estrogen or progesterone levels. What it does is support the energy systems that hormone-producing cells rely on, and reduce the cortisol response to training — which indirectly protects the hormonal environment. In perimenopause, creatine has shown particular benefit for muscle preservation as estrogen declines, because estrogen and creatine share overlapping roles in muscle energy metabolism.

The brain benefits — why this matters particularly for women

The brain uses creatine as an energy buffer in exactly the same way muscle does. Brain creatine levels correlate with cognitive performance — particularly working memory, processing speed and resistance to mental fatigue. Women have lower brain creatine concentrations than men, and this gap widens during periods of hormonal flux: the premenstrual week, postpartum, and perimenopause.

Premenstrual brain fog. The cognitive sluggishness many women experience in the late luteal phase — sometimes called premenstrual brain fog — is partly driven by reduced brain energy availability as progesterone metabolites affect GABA and serotonin systems. Creatine's role as a brain energy buffer may partially offset this. Preliminary research supports creatine supplementation improving mood and reducing depressive symptoms in women, with effects appearing more pronounced in hormonally vulnerable periods. Read more about brain fog and hormones.

Sleep deprivation. Creatine supplementation has been shown to partially offset the cognitive impairment caused by sleep deprivation — a finding that has specific relevance for women in the luteal phase, postpartum, and perimenopause, when sleep disruption is most common.

Cycle timing — when and how to take it

Creatine is effective taken daily regardless of cycle phase. Consistency matters more than timing to the cycle. However, the luteal phase is when women are most likely to notice performance and recovery benefits from supplementation, because phosphocreatine resynthesis is naturally slower and creatine compensates for that.

Dose. 3 to 5g of creatine monohydrate daily. No loading phase is required, though a loading phase (20g per day divided into four doses for 5 to 7 days) accelerates muscle saturation to the same endpoint that daily supplementation reaches in 3 to 4 weeks.

Timing. Close to training — either pre- or post-workout — appears to maximise uptake. On non-training days, timing is less important.

Form. Creatine monohydrate. Other forms — creatine HCl, ethyl ester, buffered — are marketed aggressively but have not demonstrated superiority over monohydrate in clinical research. Monohydrate is also significantly cheaper.

When in the cycle should women take creatine?
Creatine is effective taken daily regardless of cycle phase. However, research suggests women have lower phosphocreatine resynthesis rates in the luteal phase due to progesterone's effects on metabolism — making consistent daily supplementation particularly important for women. Timing relative to training matters more than cycle phase: taking creatine close to training — before or after — appears to maximise muscle creatine uptake.

The weight question — what actually happens on the scale

Creatine causes intramuscular water retention — water stored inside muscle cells, not under the skin. This is not bloating in the conventional sense. It is muscle hydration, which is structurally and metabolically desirable. Scale weight may increase by 1 to 2kg in the first week or two of supplementation.

Over time, this intracellular water retention supports protein synthesis and lean mass development. Women who track body composition rather than scale weight consistently see improvement in muscle-to-fat ratio with creatine — which is the metric that actually reflects health and hormonal function. Will creatine make women gain weight?
Creatine causes intramuscular water retention — water stored inside muscle cells, not subcutaneous bloating. This can add 1 to 2kg to scale weight in the first week or two, which is muscle hydration, not fat. Over time, this intracellular water retention supports protein synthesis and muscle growth. Women who are concerned about scale weight should track body composition rather than weight alone — creatine consistently improves muscle-to-fat ratio.
What is the best creatine for women?
Creatine monohydrate is the only form with extensive human research. Other forms — creatine HCl, buffered creatine, creatine ethyl ester — are marketed aggressively but lack the clinical evidence base of monohydrate. 3 to 5g of creatine monohydrate daily, taken consistently, is the evidence-based recommendation for women. Loading phases are optional and accelerate muscle saturation but are not necessary.
