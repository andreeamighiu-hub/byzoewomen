NAC for Women:
PCOS, Hormonal Acne and What the Research Actually Shows.

N-acetylcysteine — NAC — has been quietly accumulating an impressive body of clinical evidence for women with PCOS and androgen-driven hormonal acne. It is not a trendy supplement. It is a well-studied one. Here is what it does, how it works, and what you need to know before starting it.

By Andreea Mighiu · Zōē · byzoewomen.com

What NAC is — and why it matters hormonally

N-acetylcysteine is a precursor to glutathione — the body's most important endogenous antioxidant. Glutathione is produced in every cell and plays a central role in reducing oxidative stress, supporting liver detoxification, and regulating inflammation. When glutathione is depleted, oxidative stress rises and the cellular environment becomes less hospitable to normal hormonal function.

In women with PCOS, glutathione depletion is not incidental — it is a consistent and well-documented feature of the condition. Oxidative stress in PCOS disrupts insulin signalling, amplifies androgen production, impairs follicular development and worsens the inflammatory environment that drives many of PCOS's downstream symptoms. NAC addresses this at the root.

NAC is not a hormone. It does not act directly on estrogen, progesterone or testosterone receptors. What it does is restore the cellular conditions that allow hormonal systems to function more normally — by reducing oxidative stress, improving insulin sensitivity and supporting the liver's detoxification capacity. NAC and PCOS — what the clinical trials show

Insulin resistance. Insulin resistance is present in 50 to 70% of women with PCOS, regardless of body weight. It amplifies androgen production by stimulating the ovaries and adrenal glands to produce more testosterone, and by reducing sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG) — the protein that keeps testosterone bound and inactive. NAC improves insulin sensitivity through glutathione-mediated reduction of oxidative stress in insulin-responsive tissues.

In a landmark randomised controlled trial, NAC was compared directly to metformin — the standard pharmaceutical intervention for insulin-resistant PCOS — over 24 weeks. NAC produced comparable improvements in insulin resistance, fasting glucose and menstrual regularity, with a more favourable side effect profile. This is not a minor finding. It positions NAC as a clinically meaningful option for women who cannot tolerate metformin or prefer a non-pharmaceutical approach.

Source: Badawy et al., N-acetyl cysteine and clomiphene citrate for induction of ovulation in polycystic ovary syndrome — European Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology, 2007.

Androgen reduction. Multiple trials have demonstrated meaningful reductions in total testosterone and free androgen index in women with PCOS taking NAC. The mechanism is indirect — improved insulin sensitivity reduces the ovarian androgen excess that insulin resistance drives — but the clinical effect is real and measurable. Lower androgens translate directly to improvements in the symptoms most women with PCOS find most distressing: acne, hair loss and unwanted hair growth.

Cycle regularity and ovulation. Several trials have shown NAC improving menstrual cycle regularity and ovulation rates in anovulatory women with PCOS. In some protocols, NAC has been combined with clomiphene (a fertility medication) and shown to significantly improve ovulation and pregnancy rates compared to clomiphene alone. This is consistent with NAC's mechanism — restoring the follicular environment that chronic oxidative stress disrupts.

What does NAC do for women with PCOS?
NAC improves insulin sensitivity, reduces oxidative stress, lowers androgens and supports ovulation in women with PCOS. In clinical trials, NAC has been compared directly to metformin — the standard pharmaceutical intervention for insulin-resistant PCOS — and shown comparable improvements in insulin resistance, cycle regularity and androgen levels. It works by replenishing glutathione, the body's primary antioxidant, which is consistently depleted in PCOS.

NAC and hormonal acne — the androgen connection

Hormonal acne in women is primarily driven by androgens — specifically testosterone and its more potent derivative DHT — stimulating sebaceous glands to overproduce sebum, and by the inflammatory environment that amplifies this response. Women with PCOS have structurally elevated androgens, but androgen-driven acne can also occur in women without a formal PCOS diagnosis when insulin resistance or adrenal androgen excess is present.

NAC addresses hormonal acne through two distinct mechanisms. First, by reducing insulin resistance and the ovarian androgen excess it drives, NAC lowers the circulating androgen load reaching the skin. Second, by reducing systemic oxidative stress and inflammation — both of which amplify acne severity independently of sebum production — NAC creates a less inflammatory environment in the skin itself.

The clinical evidence for NAC in acne is growing but less extensive than its PCOS evidence base. What is clear is that in women whose acne has an androgen or insulin-resistance driver — which is the majority of adult women with persistent hormonal acne — addressing those upstream causes is more effective than topical treatments alone.

Can NAC help hormonal acne?
Yes. Hormonal acne driven by elevated androgens — particularly testosterone and DHT — is a primary feature of PCOS, and NAC's androgen-lowering effect translates directly to improvement in acne in women with androgen-driven skin issues. NAC also reduces inflammation locally, which is a key driver of acne severity beyond the hormonal component.

How to take NAC — dose, timing and what to expect

Dose. Clinical trials in PCOS have used 600mg to 1800mg per day, typically in two or three divided doses. The most common studied dose is 1200mg daily — 600mg twice daily. Starting at 600mg once daily allows tolerance to be assessed before increasing.

With food. NAC can cause nausea, particularly at higher doses and on an empty stomach. Taking it with food significantly reduces this. Some women find evening dosing better tolerated than morning.

Timeline. NAC is not a rapid-acting supplement. Insulin sensitivity improvements may appear within 4 to 8 weeks. Androgen reduction and acne improvements typically require 8 to 12 weeks of consistent use. Cycle regularity improvements are usually assessed at 24 weeks in clinical trials — the timeframe within which most meaningful hormonal changes accumulate.

Interactions. NAC should not be taken alongside nitroglycerin (used for angina) as it potentiates its vasodilatory effects. It may interact with some immunosuppressants. If you are on any prescription medications, discuss NAC with your prescribing clinician before starting.

What is the correct NAC dose for PCOS?
Clinical trials in PCOS have used doses of 600mg to 1800mg per day, typically divided into two or three doses. The most commonly studied dose is 1200mg daily (600mg twice daily). Starting at 600mg once daily and increasing gradually is advisable to assess tolerance, as NAC can cause nausea at higher doses when taken without food.
NAC does not act on hormones directly. It restores the cellular environment in which hormones can behave normally — and for women with PCOS, that distinction is everything.
How long does NAC take to work for hormonal acne and PCOS?
Clinical trials typically run for 24 weeks and show meaningful improvements in cycle regularity, androgens and ovulation over that period. Acne improvements may be visible within 8 to 12 weeks. Insulin sensitivity improvements can appear within 4 to 8 weeks. NAC is not a rapid-acting intervention — its benefits accumulate as glutathione levels are restored and oxidative stress is reduced over time.
